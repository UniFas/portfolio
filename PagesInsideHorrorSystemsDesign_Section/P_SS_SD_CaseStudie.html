2016

Layers of Fear (2016)

Layers of Fear taught me how environmental manipulation can replace enemy encounters. Its “designer controlled movement window” offered maximum tension without combat, and its psychological horror loop relies on misdirection, controlled pacing, and player-driven interpretation.

Key insights included perspective based scares, non-linear spatial loops, and mood-driven setpieces. These systems inspired how BTQ handles dread without revealing the monster too early.

  • Environment
  • Misdirection & pacing
  • Interpretation
  • No combat

2015

SOMA (2015)

SOMA showed me the power of narrative-driven horror systems. The game uses “existential friction” instead of jumpscares, letting mechanics reinforce philosophical themes.

Its monster AI supports the narrative rather than dominating it, perfect inspiration for BTQ's psychological tension loops and non-combat fear-driven progression.

  • Narrative systems
  • Monster AI
  • No combat

2012

Lucius (2012)

Lucius explores systemic mischief, where the player manipulates NPCs through item based chains. Studying it taught me how systems can reinforce theme even when the player is the threat.

I drew inspiration for BTQ’s environmental storytelling and “fear escalation” through logical events instead of combat.

  • Systemic mischief
  • Player as threat
  • Environmental storytelling

File 02 · Week 1 exercise

Fear without showing the threat.

Brief: choose a horror scene and identify three clues that make the player anticipate danger before seeing it. Deliverable: three clues + the intended player reaction.

Horror scene analysis: No, I’m Not a Human

My first playthrough of No, I'm Not a Human was different from what I expected. What stood out to me was how the game uses a relatively simple environment to create desperation and uncertainty. Rather than relying exclusively on visible threats, it encourages the player to gather information, interpret clues, and make decisions without knowing whether they are correct.

Inspect the room. The threat is never shown.

Anticipation0 / 3 clues

Illustration, not a measurement.

Tap the glowing spots in the room to read what each one tells the player.

Clue 1

Radio and television broadcasts

The radio and television communicate information about the danger outside. These broadcasts allow the player to understand that something is wrong without necessarily seeing the threat directly.

Intended player reactionConcern, curiosity, and anticipation of danger beyond the immediate environment.

Clue 2

Windows and the outside world

The windows establish a boundary between the apparent safety of the house and the unknown outside. They remind the player that danger might exist beyond their immediate surroundings, even when nothing threatening is visible.

Intended player reactionSuspicion, apprehension, and a desire to check what might be outside.

Clue 3

Telephone interactions and limited information

The telephone provides another way to gather information. However, the player must interpret what they learn alongside other clues and make decisions without complete certainty. When interactions are constrained by limited resources, obtaining more information also becomes a decision in itself.

Intended player reactionCuriosity mixed with anxiety about whether the information is sufficient to make the right decision.

My main takeaway

What I found particularly effective was the uncertainty surrounding the people the player allows into the house. I was left wondering whether the people I had saved were actually human and whether I could trust my own judgement.

For me, the fear came not only from the possibility of danger, but from the responsibility of making decisions without knowing the truth. The game demonstrates how information, environmental boundaries, and uncertainty can create dread before a threat is directly revealed.
