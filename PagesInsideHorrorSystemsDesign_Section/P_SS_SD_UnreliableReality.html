01

Where this started

This project started with a short video. It showed a mechanic I loved: objects in a horror space that change when you stop looking at them. The creator was selling it as an asset for €11.

My reaction was simple: I can do better.

I don't mean cheaper or flashier. I mean a different kind of thing. A pack gives you an object that moves when you look away. I wanted a system that decides when an anomaly is worth triggering, why it was chosen, and what the player gets back afterwards (relief). One designer can open, tune, test and understand it.

North star Create deliberate rhythms of anticipation, pressure, uncertainty and relief. The system chooses meaningful events, respects pacing constraints, and explains every decision to the designer. It does not scare the player as often as possible.
02

The problem I'm solving

Most horror triggers are timers with a dice roll attached. When the timer expires, something happens. That makes scares predictable, noisy and sometimes unfair: an event lands right after a high-pressure moment, or while the player can see exactly how the trick works.

Too predictable

Events always happen at the same moments, so the player anticipates them.

Too random

Events ignore what the player has recently experienced.

The goal

Unpredictable to the player, controllable to the designer.

Predictable System → Unpredictable Experience

Design question How can a horror game build a deliberate rhythm of anticipation, pressure and relief, while keeping the player's trust and making the director's decisions understandable to the designer?
Hypothesis (not a result) If the system weighs observation, uncertainty, exposure, recent pressure and recovery, and enforces prerequisites, cooldowns and exclusions, then events will feel more intentional and less repetitive than those driven by a timer or flat probability. That is a hypothesis. I will validate it with playtests rather than assume it.

Design principles

PrincipleWhat it means in practice
Unreliable, not constantly activeObjects can change while unseen, but an eligible moment does not have to produce an event.
Pacing over volumeAfter heavy pressure, prefer a quieter anomaly or a recovery window over automatic escalation.
Fairness through consistencyEvents follow stable rules. They can surprise, but the system never ignores its own constraints.
Designer visibilityDebug tools show eligibility, selection, approval and rejection reasons.
Proxies, not mind-readingTension variables are game-state signals. I test the player's actual response separately.

What the player should feel

  • AnticipationSomething might have changed while I was looking away.
  • UncertaintyI'm not sure this space is reliable.
  • AgencyI can inspect, track and avoid, but I can't fully control the room.
  • ReliefThe game lets the pressure settle. It isn't constantly punishing me.

Two bugs worth documenting

The line trace that couldn't see its own target

The visibility trace had Ignore Self enabled, so it skipped the very object it was trying to detect. Unchecking it fixed observation. It is a small bug, but exactly the kind a case study should record.

The Director reference was None

GetActorOfClass returned nothing usable in this setup, which caused a runtime error. The fix: expose HorrorDirector as Instance Editable, assign the level instance to each object, and validate before use. Automatic registration comes later.

The architectural leap: the Director decides

Right now, each object asks the Director for permission. That is the prototype. The real system flips it: objects report their state, and the Director chooses one eligible object and one allowed event. That ends the competition between objects and puts every pacing rule in one place.

  2. 02Object requests an event"Can I perform this anomaly?"
  3. 03Horror Director evaluatesTension · cooldowns · randomness

Every object competes. Pacing rules would have to be repeated or scattered.

An object does not simply decide "I am going to scare the player now." It reports what it could do. Keeping horror behaviours decoupled from the global tension logic means new objects become clients of the same system without rewriting the Director.

05

Rules, states and exclusions

Object states

StateMeaningMoves on when
ObservedIn the player's view cone and range, with clear line of sight.Player looks away.
Unobserved TrackingUnseen timer is accumulating.Delay and cooldown are satisfied.
EligibleObject and global requirements all pass.Director selects it, or it keeps waiting.
Event PendingRequest approved; duplicates blocked.Event begins.
ExecutingThe event is in progress.Event completes or cancels.
Cooldown / RecoveryObject and/or system rests.Cooldown ends and other rules pass.
Disabled / InvalidNot permitted, or reference lost.Re-enabled or re-registered.

Approval layers

Probability is evaluated only after all mandatory checks pass.

  1. Global gatesevents enabled, tension in range, Director state valid.
  2. Object gatesregistered, valid, selectable, unobserved, delay met, cooldown passed, not executing.
  3. Event prerequisitesobject supports the event, destination is usable, no incompatible event active.
  4. Pacing constraintsnot right after an intense threat, no active recovery window, not too many recent anomalies.
  5. Safety constraintsnot in a protected safe zone, not during a scripted sequence, not into a blocked destination.
Approved

Event can occur

The current tension, timing and context allow the event to contribute to the intended experience.

Denied

Nothing happens

The request is rejected because the timing would create too much pressure, repeat an event too quickly, or simply be less effective.

Denial is invisible to the player. They simply experience a world in which sometimes nothing happens. That absence is part of the system.

Action versus dramatic role

I keep two separate labels. What the object does (Move, DisappearReappear, Rotate, Scale) is different from what the moment is for in the pacing.

Dramatic categoryPurpose
AnticipationSuggest danger without a major payoff, such as a distant shift or a light change.
AnomalyUndermine confidence in the environment: disappear, rotate, scale, relocate.
Direct ThreatImmediate danger. Only after anomaly pacing is stable.
RecoveryA quiet window, reduced probability, a neutral environment.

Anti-repetition

  • Avoid selecting the same object repeatedly when other suitable candidates exist.
  • Track recent categories and avoid repeating one too often.
  • Enforce a maximum consecutive-pressure rule, so a direct threat is followed by a recovery opportunity.
  • Show every exclusion in debug output. Nothing is bypassed silently.

First selection rules

  1. Discard invalid references.
  2. Reject objects that are not selectable, already executing, or currently observed.
  3. Reject objects that haven't met their unseen-time and cooldown requirements.
  4. Build the list of event types both the object and the Director allow.
  5. If nothing is eligible, do nothing and log NO_CANDIDATES.
  6. Pick one candidate uniformly at random. Add ObjectWeight only after uniform selection is solid.
  7. Execute the event, then record object, event and timestamp.

I'm keeping this deliberately small: registry first, then print the count, then select one object, then trigger its existing disappear/reappear. Weighted selection and advanced tension logic come only after that slice passes.

06

The tension model

CurrentTension is currently a controllable value. The next step is deriving it from game conditions. These signals are gameplay proxies, not a measurement of the player's emotions. It is a designer-facing control signal that says how much pressure the system should be applying.

SignalQuestion it answersExample inputs
ExposureHow vulnerable is the player right now?Dangerous area, no cover, threat nearby.
UncertaintyHow incomplete or unreliable is the information?Threat not visible, contradictory clues, displaced familiar object.
Recent PressureHow intense has the last stretch been?Recent chase or close call, short time since a high-intensity event.
RecoveryHas the player had room to settle?Time since pressure, safe area access, low recent event count.

First scoring model

BasePressure = (Exposure * ExposureWeight)
             + (Uncertainty * UncertaintyWeight)
             + (RecentPressure * RecentPressureWeight)

TensionTarget = Clamp(BasePressure - RecoveryRelief, 0, 1) * 100
CurrentTension moves gradually toward TensionTarget

This is a starting point. I'll display the raw inputs first, tune the weights by hand, and smooth the movement so no single signal causes a jarring swing.

Tuning bands (proposed)

Calm0–24Rare or no anomalies. Exploration and relief.
Building25–49Anticipation and low-intensity anomalies.
High50–74Stronger anomalies eligible, subject to cooldowns.
Critical75–100Don't auto-maximise. Withhold, or deliver a meaningful payoff, then recover.

Only the minimum threshold and the maximum bound are implemented today. The bands are the plan.

Recovery is a rule, not just a subtraction. After a chase, the Director can choose silence or a quiet anomaly over another attack. That is what makes the pacing deliberate.

    Testing

    Results pending

    Every player-experience claim stays a hypothesis until it's been tested and recorded. I only add a result here once I've written it down.

    Technical test matrix

    TestSetupExpected
    VisibleIn range, in view, unobstructed.Observed true; timer resets.
    Outside viewTurn away, stay near.Observed false; timer grows.
    BlockedWall between camera and object.Not reported as observed.
    Distance limitMove past ObservationDistance.Not observed.
    Probability boundsTest 0 and 1 repeatedly.0 never approves; 1 always passes the roll.
    Missing DirectorClear the reference.No crash; clear warning.
    ReappearanceTrigger unseen, look back at different points.Object relocates and returns; no visible teleport under intended conditions.
    Multiple objectsThree or more candidates, debug on.One event at a time; selection explainable in the log.

    Player test protocol

    1. Run a short, unannounced exploration with a few unreliable objects.
    2. Afterwards, ask what they noticed, when, and what they thought caused it.
    3. Collect 1 to 7 ratings for tension, predictability, fairness and recovery. These are subjective reports, not fear measurements.
    4. Repeat with a second configuration: frequent events versus longer recovery and stronger exclusions.
    5. Compare across participants. Never conclude from one.

    Key questions: did an event feel earned, random, repetitive or unfair? Was there enough quiet after tension? When did you stop trusting the environment?

    Risks I'm planning for

    RiskMitigation
    Too many per-frame line tracesProfile first, then throttle or stagger observation checks.
    Actor reference is NoneInstance Editable plus IsValid checks now; central registration later.
    Hidden actor still collidesTest it; deliberately disable and restore collision where needed.
    Reappearance inside geometryDesigner-set offsets first; trace and floor validation or authored targets later.
    Player looks back mid-eventDefine the policy explicitly: cancel, reverse, finish, or complete hidden.
    Simultaneous triggersCentral selection, bEventPending, a global event lock, cooldowns.
    Zero-probability edge caseUse a strict comparison or explicit 0/1 handling, then retest.
    Noisy debug logsDebugMode toggle, reason codes, short recent-event history.
    Tension mistaken for fearCall the signals proxies and validate with player feedback.

    Non-goals for the first slice

    • No full adaptive AI director before central selection works.
    • No building all four actions at once. One reliable action first.
    • No claim that the system detects fear.
    • No premature optimisation. Baseline first, then profile.
    11

    Why this matters for horror design

    The interesting part isn't that an object can move or change. Those effects are relatively simple.

    The difficult question is:
    "When should the game allow the player to notice?"

    That question pushed the project beyond individual scares and into designing the rules governing when horror is allowed to happen.

    First vertical slice: success criteria

    • At least three registered objects with distinct per-instance settings.
    • At least two reliable actions: Move and Disappear/Reappear.
    • The Director selects one eligible object and never requests an unsupported event.
    • Cooldown and recovery constraints are observable and testable.
    • Debug output explains each major selection or rejection.
    • A small playtest compares two pacing configurations and records the results.
    Portfolio story I built a director that makes environmental horror deliberate and tuneable. It began as an observation-gated object anomaly and is becoming a centralised event selector with pacing constraints and designer-facing diagnostics.

    The goal isn't to make objects behave strangely. Anyone can do that for €11. The goal is a horror pacing system a designer can understand, tune, test and improve, with the evidence to show every decision made the experience better.
